# Where outperformance can come from, how large it is, and how certain it is

**Question.** Given what the [portfolio edge framework](portfolio-edge-research-framework.md)
has already established, where can a real retail investor's net return actually exceed a
stated benchmark, how large is each source, and how certain is each one?

**Decision informed.** This page fixes the *size and certainty class* of every return
source the repository is willing to model, and the benchmark each one is measured
against. It settles open question 2 of the framework — whether expected log wealth is
"an arbitrary nonlinear transformation of wealth" — because a sizing engine cannot be
built without an objective. Choosing products, giving advice, and forecasting any
particular market are out of scope.

**Conclusion, stated directly.**

1. **The volatility-harvesting premise is mathematically true and practically small.** A
   constant-weight portfolio of assets with equal long-run growth does asymptotically
   beat holding any one of them, and beats an untouched buy-and-hold portfolio of the
   same assets. The exact condition is proved below: a constant-weight portfolio beats
   buy-and-hold asymptotically **if and only if `g_p > max_i g_i`**. But at any human
   horizon almost all of the gain accrues to *owning both assets*, not to *rebalancing
   them*. For a 60/40 portfolio the excess growth rate is 32.7 bp/yr, of which a 30-year
   buy-and-hold captures 30.3 bp for free, leaving **2.4 bp/yr** in expectation for
   rebalancing as a policy.
2. **"Near definitively" is refuted quantitatively.** The probability that continuous
   rebalancing beats buy-and-hold has a closed form that depends on volatility,
   correlation and horizon only through the product `c = gamma_star * T`. It never falls
   below **68.27%** and reaches 90% only at `c ≈ 3.90` — **390 years** at the most
   favourable plausible excess growth rate of 100 bp/yr. Rebalancing is a short straddle
   on relative performance with a premium that grows like `T` against a payoff that
   grows like `sqrt(T)`. Confidence does accrue. It accrues at a rate no investor can
   use.
3. **The premise is right that outperformance is possible, and wrong about which
   benchmark.** Against a cheap index, the whole honest budget is about **24 bp/yr
   central, with a range from −30 to +101 bp**, and a 30-year probability of coming out
   ahead of about **0.63**; 90% confidence would need about 443 years. Against the
   investor's own plausible alternative — the average actively managed dollar, held
   without tax management — the budget is about **89 bp/yr against 41 bp of tracking
   error**, which reaches 99% confidence in **fourteen months**. The achievable,
   near-certain outperformance is against *your own counterfactual and the average
   investor*, never against the index.
4. **The Chambers–Zdanowicz dismissal is wrong as stated and right in what it defends.**
   Their zero-expected-profit theorem is correct and generalises. But the log advantage
   is not an artefact of a utility function: `(1/T) log W_T` is a pathwise property of
   the realised wealth path with no preferences in it. What a log investor gains is
   exactly a **mean-preserving contraction** — identical expected terminal wealth,
   materially lower variance, higher median. And their own Exhibit 5, extended past the
   12 periods at which they stopped, has the gap growing without bound.

## 1. The theory, settled by derivation

Two assets follow correlated geometric Brownian motions with log-drifts `g_i`,
volatilities `sigma_i`, correlation `rho`. Write `X_i(T)` for the log price relative,
`D(T) = X_a(T) − X_b(T)`, and `tau**2 = sigma_a**2 + sigma_b**2 − 2 rho sigma_a sigma_b`.

### 1.1 Verified facts

**The excess growth rate.** For constant long-only weights,
`g_p = sum_i w_i g_i + gamma_star` with
`gamma_star = 0.5 (sum_i w_i sigma_i**2 − sigma_p**2) >= 0`. At equal volatilities and
50/50 this is `sigma**2 (1 − rho) / 4 = tau**2 / 8`. This is the framework's existing
statement ([Willenbrock 2011](https://arxiv.org/abs/1109.1256)), re-derived rather than
copied; the subtrahend `sum_i w_i g_i` is not the return of any investable portfolio.

**Buy-and-hold's asymptotic growth is `max_i g_i`, almost surely.** For `n` assets with
fixed positive weights,
`M(T) + log(min_i w_i) <= log sum_i w_i e**X_i(T) <= M(T)` where `M(T) = max_i X_i(T)`,
because the sum is at least `w_j e**M` for the maximising `j` and at most `e**M` since
the weights sum to one. Both bounds are `M(T) + O(1)`, so
`(1/T) log V_hold(T) − M(T)/T -> 0`; the strong law gives `X_i(T)/T -> g_i`. **A
buy-and-hold portfolio converges on its single best component and asymptotically throws
away the whole of `gamma_star`.**

**The exact condition.** A constant-weight portfolio beats buy-and-hold asymptotically
(in almost-sure growth rate) **iff `g_p > max_i g_i`**, i.e.
`sum_i w_i g_i + gamma_star > max_i g_i`. The orchestrating hypothesis was correct. With
equal drifts this reduces to `gamma_star > 0`, which holds for any long-only weights on
assets not perfectly co-moving in log space — so with equal drifts rebalancing *always*
wins eventually.

**The pathwise identity, and why rebalancing is a short straddle.** For equal
volatilities at 50/50, `0.5(e**u + e**v) = e**((u+v)/2) cosh((u−v)/2)` gives, with no
approximation,

```
log V_reb(T) − log V_hold(T) = gamma_star * T − log cosh(D(T)/2).
```

The common factor cancels **pathwise**. Since `log cosh(d/2) -> |d|/2 − log 2`, this is
literally the payoff of a short straddle on relative log performance, struck at zero,
with premium `gamma_star * T`. That derives the qualitative
[Rattray et al. (2020)](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P145_Strategic_rebalancing.pdf)
two-period identity in continuous time, and explains its shape: the upside is capped at
`gamma_star * T`, the downside is not capped at all.

**One function governs rebalancing frequency, buy-and-hold and continuous rebalancing.**
With `B(v) = E[log cosh(Z/2)]`, `Z ~ N(0, v)`: rebalancing at interval `h` earns
`B(tau**2 h)/h` per year, buy-and-hold for `T` years earns `B(tau**2 T)/T`, and
continuous rebalancing earns `tau**2/8`. Monthly rebalancing captures 99.917% of the
continuous bonus and annual 99.026%, so **rebalancing frequency is a second-order
question and horizon is a first-order one**.

**The probability has a closed form.** From the identity, the event is
`|D| < 2 arccosh(e**c)` with `c = gamma_star T` and `D ~ N(0, 8c)`, so

```
P(rebalanced beats buy-and-hold) = 2 * Phi( 2 arccosh(e**c) / sqrt(8c) ) − 1.
```

It depends on volatility, correlation and horizon **only through `c`**.

**The break-even drift gap is horizon-free.** Against the *ex-ante* higher-drift asset
(log-drift `g + delta` versus `g − delta`),
`P = Phi((gamma_star − delta) sqrt(T / (2 gamma_star)))`. At `delta = gamma_star` this is
exactly 0.5 at **every** horizon; above it, rebalancing loses with probability
approaching one.

### 1.2 The numbers these produce

Two 20%-volatility assets with identical log-drift, 50/50, 30 years, monthly
rebalancing. All closed form; the seeded 20,000-path Monte Carlo agrees within three of
its own standard errors.

| rho | `gamma_star` | Buy-and-hold captures | Rebalancing residual (mean) | Median | 5th pct | P(rebal wins) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.0 | 100.0 bp | 81.69 bp | **18.31 bp** | 56.44 bp | −190.6 bp | 0.7066 |
| 0.3 | 70.0 bp | 59.97 bp | 10.03 bp | 39.12 bp | −147.7 bp | 0.6995 |
| 0.6 | 40.0 bp | 36.26 bp | 3.74 bp | 22.12 bp | −94.6 bp | 0.6923 |
| 0.9 | 10.0 bp | 9.72 bp | 0.28 bp | 5.47 bp | −27.0 bp | 0.6851 |

Three readings that matter more than the table.

- **The win probability is nearly inert.** It moves from 0.685 to 0.707 across the whole
  correlation range, because its floor as `c -> 0` is `2 Phi(1) − 1 = 68.27%`. A 70% win
  rate against buy-and-hold is the *null*, not evidence.
- **The mean and the median differ by a factor of three.** `log cosh` is convex in `|D|`
  and `|D|` is right-skewed, so the expected-log advantage (18.3 bp) is far below the
  median advantage (56.4 bp). Reporting only the mean understates a typical path;
  reporting only the median hides a 5th percentile at −191 bp/yr over thirty years.
- **Realistic portfolios are far smaller.** A 60/40 at `sigma = 16%/6%`, `rho = 0.1` has
  `gamma_star = 32.74 bp`, of which buy-and-hold captures 30.29 bp, leaving **2.45 bp/yr**
  in the mean and 17.9 bp at the median, `P = 0.692`.

**More assets makes buy-and-hold capture *more*, not less.** One hundred equicorrelated
stocks at `sigma = 30%`, `rho = 0.25` have `gamma_star = 334 bp/yr`. Over 30 years a
buy-and-hold portfolio of the same hundred captures more than 95% of it, leaving about
**4 bp/yr** for rebalancing. Dispersion across many names makes the untouched portfolio's
log-sum-exp large. So an equal-weight sleeve run against a cap-weighted index is not
harvesting 334 bp of volatility; whatever it earns is a size and value tilt, which the
framework's factor evidence governs, not this page.

**The estimation cliff.** With `gamma_star = 100 bp`, a drift gap `delta` moves the
30-year probability of beating the ex-ante better asset from 0.651 at `delta = 0` to
exactly 0.500 at `delta = 100 bp` and 0.123 at `delta = 400 bp`. A 1%/yr difference in
true expected log return between two broad equity markets is far inside the standard
error of anything estimable from data, and it exactly cancels the whole effect.

**Horizons to confidence, at the most favourable plausible `gamma_star` of 100 bp/yr:**
75% at 88 years, 80% at 163, **90% at 390**, 95% at 622. At a realistic 40 bp/yr,
multiply by 2.5.

### 1.3 Chambers and Zdanowicz: settling open question 2

Retrieved and fully recomputed:
[Chambers and Zdanowicz (2014)](https://www.hec.ca/finance/Fichier/Chambers2014.pdf),
"The Limitations of Diversification Return", *Journal of Portfolio Management* 40(4).

**What they prove, verified.** Their Exhibit 4 fixture — two assets, ±25%/−20% at
`p = 0.5`, uncorrelated, equal weights, two periods, 16 paths — reproduces exactly:
`E[W_T] = 1.050625` for both policies, and their long-rebalanced/short-buy-and-hold trade
has **exactly zero** expected profit. Verbatim: *"There is zero expected profit to an
arbitrager from being long the higher average geometric mean strategy (rebalancing) and
being short the lower average geometric mean strategy (buy and hold)."*

The result also **generalises**, which strengthens their case. For returns independent
across time, `E[W_reb] = (1 + w'mu)**T` and `E[W_hold] = sum_i w_i (1 + mu_i)**T`. These
coincide when all `mu_i` are equal, and by strict convexity of `x -> (1 + x)**T`
buy-and-hold is strictly *ahead* on expected terminal wealth whenever the means differ —
with means of 10% and 2% over 30 periods, 9.630 against 5.743, a 68% advantage. In
continuous time the equality is exact too: `E[V_T] = e**(mu T)` for both policies at every
horizon and every correlation. **An investor who genuinely maximises expected terminal
wealth should never rebalance.**

**Three corrections to what is currently written about them.**

1. **The framework mislabels their headline figures.** Their 1.874% and 1.867% are
   `E[W_T**(1/T)] − 1`, the *expected annualised compound rate*. Expected log wealth per
   period is 1.2346% and 1.2201%. Both metrics rank the policies the same way and both
   favour rebalancing, so the framework's conclusion survives, but the numbers quoted
   beside it belong to a different statistic.
2. **Their "arbitrary nonlinear transformation" sentence is about the annualised rate,
   not about log wealth.** Verbatim: *"the controversy relates to whether an investor
   should be more concerned about expected long term growth expressed as an expected
   annualized rate or expressed as an expected total percentage change in value. The
   former is an arbitrary nonlinear transformation of wealth, the latter is not."* On its
   own terms this is a fair objection: `E[W**(1/T)]` averages *rates across paths*, which
   is a strange operation. It is not an objection to `E[log W_T]`, which they never
   analyse.
3. **Two arithmetic slips in their Exhibit 5.** Recomputation reproduces 34 of 36 printed
   figures exactly. The 12-period expected value is printed as $1.3499 where their own
   text requires `1.025**12 = $1.34489`; and the 4-period buy-and-hold mean return is
   printed as 1.52% where recomputation gives 1.5271%, which rounds to 1.53%. Neither
   changes a conclusion.

**Where they stop is where the argument turns.** Their footnote says the horizon "was not
extended beyond 12 years because a four path tree with non-recombining nodes" becomes
unmanageable. It does not have to be enumerated: both policies are recombining and the
whole exhibit computes in `O(T**2)`. Extended:

| Periods | Rebalanced rate | Buy-and-hold rate | Gap | Rebalanced `E[log W]/T` | Held `E[log W]/T` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 12 (their last row) | 1.3479% | 1.2299% | 0.118 pp | 1.2346% | 1.1088% |
| 100 | 1.2550% | 0.7739% | 0.481 pp | 1.2346% | 0.7553% |
| 400 | 1.2455% | 0.4869% | 0.759 pp | 1.2346% | 0.4815% |
| 3,000 | 1.2427% | 0.2088% | 1.034 pp | 1.2346% | 0.2080% |
| limit | 1.2423% | **0.0000%** | 1.242 pp | 1.2346% | 0.0000% |

The rebalanced portfolio's expected log growth is **constant at 1.2346% at every
horizon** — log wealth is additive, so the per-period expectation cannot depend on `T` —
while buy-and-hold's falls monotonically to `max_i g_i`, which is exactly 0% here because
`sqrt(1.25 × 0.80) = 1`. Their 12-period gap of 12 bp is not the size of the effect; it is
the size of the effect at the horizon they stopped at.

**Verdict.** *The dismissal is wrong as stated and right in what it defends.*

- Wrong, because the advantage is not created by applying a utility function.
  `(1/T) log W_T` is the realised growth rate of the actual wealth path, and it converges
  almost surely to the quantity the log expectation computes. Log is also the unique
  criterion with an asymptotic dominance theorem:
  [Breiman (1961)](https://projecteuclid.org/ebook/Download?urlId=bsmsp%2F1200512159&isFullBook=false),
  §3, verbatim — *"asymptotically, S\*ₙ is as large as the Sₙ provided by any strategy Λ,
  and if Λ is not asymptotically close to Λ\*, then S\*ₙ is infinitely larger than Sₙ"*,
  formalised as Theorem 2: *"Let Λ be any strategy leading to the fortune Sₙ after n
  plays. Then limₙ Sₙ/S\*ₙ exists a.s. and E(limₙ Sₙ/S\*ₙ) ≤ 1."* No other utility has
  that property.
- Right, because expected terminal wealth is a legitimate alternative objective, and
  under it rebalancing is worth nothing. Their own deciding example prices the
  disagreement cleanly: an 18-year deposit paying a guaranteed 4% is worth $20,258.17 per
  $10,000; a coin flip between 0% and 8% is worth $24,980.10 in expectation — 23.3% more —
  but its log certainty equivalent is $19,990.05. **A log investor pays $268.12 per
  $10,000, 1.32%, to decline a gamble whose expected value is 23% higher.** That is a
  preference, not an error, and Samuelson's objection to geometric-mean maximisation at
  finite horizons stands untouched.

**What a log-utility investor actually gains** is therefore exactly a mean-preserving
contraction. At `mu = 7%`, `sigma = 20%`, `rho = 0`, `T = 30`: expected terminal wealth
8.1662 for **both**; variance 54.82 rebalanced against 77.36 held, a **29.1% variance
reduction at an unchanged mean**; median 6.0496 against 5.6610. That is the whole economic
content of the diversification return, and it is worth having — but it is diversification,
priced in single-digit-to-tens of basis points of growth, not an arbitrage.

**Consequence: the repository should declare net geometric growth (equivalently expected
log wealth) as its objective, and must state it as a declared preference rather than as a
proof.** The asymptotic theorems justify it; they do not compel it at a finite horizon.

### 2.1 What survives, with its evidence

**Cost reduction — the only large, deterministic line.**
[Sharpe (1991)](https://web.stanford.edu/~wfsharpe/art/active/active.htm) is the
backbone, verbatim: *"before costs, the return on the average actively managed dollar will
equal the return on the average passively managed dollar"* and *"after costs, the return
on the average actively managed dollar will be less than the return on the average
passively managed dollar… they depend only on the laws of addition, subtraction,
multiplication and division."* Sizes, as of 2025 year-end data:
[Morningstar's 2026 US Fund Fee Study](https://www.morningstar.com/content/cs-assets/v3/assets/blt9415ea4cc4157833/bltceffe0d68d048b0a/6a0cd665d885fdf4113e0ed0/US_Fund_Fee_Study_Q2_2026.pdf)
(published May 2026, vendor research) gives an asset-weighted 0.09% for broad index funds
against 0.57% for active, and 0.58% for active US equity specifically;
[ICI's *Trends in the Expenses and Fees of Funds, 2025*](https://www.ici.org/files/2026/per32-01.pdf)
(March 2026) gives 0.05% for index equity mutual funds against a 0.64% industry average
for actively managed domestic equity mutual funds. The gap is 48–59 bp. **Use the
asset-weighted figures**: the equal-weighted active-equity average is 1.00–1.12%, which
overstates what investors actually pay by roughly 45 bp.

Two limits that are usually dropped. First, the saving is measured against *the fund the
investor would otherwise have held*, not against the index — an index fund can never beat
its own index by cutting fees. Second, switching from one randomly chosen active fund to an
index fund carries the *active fund's* idiosyncratic risk: at 350 bp of tracking error a
50 bp fee edge is only 78% likely to be ahead after 30 years, and needs 81 years for 90%
confidence. Against the *average active dollar* — a diversified pool — the tracking error
collapses and the saving is near-certain. **The certainty is a property of the pairing, not
of the fee.**

**Sharpe's identity is not exact, and the correction cuts both ways.**
[Pedersen (2018)](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/60084093/lasse_heje_pedersen_sharpening_the_arithmetic_of_active_management_publishersversion.pdf)
(FAJ 74(1), CC BY; author is an AQR principal) shows the identity assumes the market
portfolio never changes, verbatim: *"new shares are issued, others are repurchased, and
indexes are reconstituted—so even 'passive' investors must regularly trade."* US equity
turnover averaged **7.6%/yr over 1926–2015**, of which 5.3 pp is listings, SEOs, delistings
and repurchases. He estimates the passive investor's implicit cost from IPO underpricing at
about 18 bp and from SEOs at about 6 bp, and writes: *"if you pay a manager 10 bps to track
the S&P 500, you could be paying an explicit 10 bps on top of an implicit cost of more than
25 bps."* The independent measurement is
[Petajisto (2011)](https://www.petajisto.net/papers/petajisto%202011%20jef%20-%20hidden%20cost%20for%20index%20funds.pdf)
(*Journal of Empirical Finance* 18(2), 271–288 — it is frequently miscited to *Journal of
Financial Markets* 14(4)): **21–28 bp/yr for S&P 500
index funds and 38–77 bp/yr for Russell 2000 funds, 1990–2005**, peaking at 65–82 bp and
232–463 bp respectively in 2000.

**This line is deliberately not booked, because the effect has since disappeared.**
Petajisto's own sub-period table already halves it, to 14–19 bp and 14–29 bp over
2001–2005. Greenwood and Sammon, "The Disappearing Index Effect", *Journal of Finance* 80(2),
657–698 (2025) — published text unreachable, [working paper at NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30748) —
published abstract verbatim: *"The abnormal return associated with a stock being added to
the S&P 500 has fallen from an average of 7.4% in the 1990s to less than 1% over the past
decade… with large negative abnormal returns during the 1990s but an average return of
only 0.1% between 2010 and 2020."* Bennett, Stulz and Wang
([NBER w27593](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27593)) find the same independently: the
[−5, +5] abnormal return falls from 4.7–4.9% over 1997–2007 to an **insignificant** 0.6–0.7%
over 2008–2017. Anyone citing Petajisto's headline as a current cost is off by roughly an
order of magnitude. *(Retrieval note: the published Greenwood–Sammon text was unreachable;
the abstract above is from Crossref metadata. Three working-paper versions circulate with
different figures — 7.6% and 7.3% for the 1990s — and a widely repeated "0.3%" appears in
none of them.)*

**The behaviour gap is real but roughly ten times smaller than advertised.** The vendor
headline is
[Morningstar's *Mind the Gap 2026*](https://www.morningstar.com/content/cs-assets/v3/assets/blt9415ea4cc4157833/blt852a4301adc2f5db/6a7373d51f7b5ae2da78e1e8/US_Mind_the_Gap_2026.pdf)
(published 2026-08-06): 8.7% investor return against 9.9% total return over the ten years
to 2025-12-31, a **1.2 pp/yr** gap. Morningstar's own caveats are unusually candid and
should be quoted rather than the headline: *"it's not advisable to view this study's
findings as a parable of 'dumb money'"*, and *"even laudable practices like investing a
portion of every paycheck or regularly rebalancing can open a gap"*. The peer-reviewed
refutation is decisive: Fulkerson, Jordan, Riley and Yan, *"Bad Timing Does Not Cost
Investors 15% of Their Funds' Returns"*,
[*Financial Analysts Journal* 82(3) (2026)](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2026/bad-timing-does-not-cost-investors-funds-returns),
verbatim: *"we find that, using the same sample, poor timing by mutual fund
investors costs them only 0.10% per year."* **The paper is open access and has now been
read in full** (2026-08-12; Taylor & Francis blocks direct fetch, so it was read through a
text-extraction proxy). Its Table 2 applies
[Hayley (2014, *JFQA* 49(1))](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6226/1/Measuring%20Investors%20Historical%20Returns%20-%20March%202012.pdf)'s
decomposition to Morningstar's own 2015–2024 sample and splits an overall **−0.17 pp** gap
into **−0.10 pp of timing and −0.07 pp of hindsight**, with the same near-even split in
every category group. Hayley's original decomposition of Dichev's 1.3 pp NYSE/AMEX gap is
harsher still: **−0.21 pp of timing against −0.95 pp of bias**.

**Two cautions the authors state and citations usually drop.** Their −0.17 pp is not
Morningstar's −1.2 pp: implementing Hayley on monthly data forces them to *estimate*
terminal assets, and that change alone removes most of the gap, so they claim only that
*"substantial portions of the Return Gaps for aggregate investors are consistently not
related to timing."* And the genuinely mechanical finding sits in their Appendix A,
concerning Morningstar's **numerator** rather than its IRR: weighting by end-of-month
rather than beginning-of-month assets overstates total return, because *"the portfolio is
weighted using the uninvestable end-of-month assets."* Correcting it moves the measured
gap from **−2.14% to −1.64% to −0.93%/yr** across Morningstar's 2023, 2024 and 2025
methods — in the alternatives group, from −5.54% to −0.06%. Morningstar had already
adopted the corrected method by the 2025 edition. **A large share of the historical
behaviour-gap literature is measuring a weighting convention.**

Fund-level evidence bounds the upper end: Friesen and Sapp (2007, *JBF* 31(9)), full text
verified, report *"equity fund investor timing decisions reduce fund investor average
returns by 1.56% annually"* over 1991–2004, **but** that is an equal-weighted mean across
7,125 share classes, and their own index-fund subsample shows a gap of only 0.05%/month —
0.60%/yr. The budget therefore books **5–60 bp with a central 15 bp**, not the 120–150 bp
that circulates. That line is booked against the *average investor* and **does not join
the contractual budget**; §2.4 settles why, and why the gap is identically zero for an
investor who does not trade.

**DALBAR must not be cited, and the defect is now pinned to a specific step.** Its
[live QAIB glossary](https://blog.dalbar.com/qaib-glossary) (retrieved 2026-08-12, and
word-identical to the QAIB 2016, 2022 and 2023 report PDFs) defines investor return as
*"the change in assets, after excluding sales, redemptions, and exchanges"* and annualises
by *"the uniform rate that can be compounded annually"*. The QAIB 2016 report's own worked
appendix shows what that means arithmetically: step 4 forms a "cost basis" by **adding up
undiscounted contributions** — opening assets, plus new investments, minus withdrawals,
plus and minus exchanges — step 5 divides dollar gain by that sum, and step 6 annualises
with a plain root, *"[% Return ^(1/# of years)]-1"*. **There is no discounting of dated
cash flows anywhere in the calculation.** It is not a mis-specified IRR; it is not an IRR.

Three independent critics reach the same place, and none is peer-reviewed.
Pfau, "A Warning to the Advisory Profession: DALBAR's Math is Wrong", *Advisor
Perspectives*, 6 March 2017 (the canonical URL returns HTTP 403; read via the Internet
Archive) shows the consequence: *"DALBAR ignores dollar-cost averaging component of these systematic
investments and instead assumes that the entire $10,000 was invested at the beginning of
1997"*, so a saver who simply did not yet have the money is scored as having mistimed. On
his worked 1997–2016 example the true dollar-cost-averaging IRR is 7.34%/yr and DALBAR's
method returns 4.05% — a 3.3 pp gap that is pure methodology and zero behaviour.
[Edesess, "Investors Do Not Underperform their Investments"](https://web.archive.org/web/20220125045454id_/https://www.advisorperspectives.com/articles/2017/10/09/investors-do-not-underperform-their-investments),
9 October 2017, names step 5 as *"the fatal flaw"* and builds the cleanest possible
counterexample: an investor who contributes the same amount every month and earns the same
return every month is scored by DALBAR as underperforming by **6.43%/yr**, which is the
same artefact §2.4 reproduces from first principles. And Blanchett — then at Morningstar,
writing in *Advisor Perspectives*, 8 May 2017 — replicated the exercise on fund data and
concluded *"DALBAR's numbers are wrong and should not be cited in the context in which
they are commonly used"*, measuring average rolling five-year gaps of **−0.32%, −0.16% and
−0.23%** for equity, fixed income and allocation funds.

**The absence of a refereed critique was checked rather than assumed.** Crossref and
OpenAlex return no journal article critiquing QAIB; the only DALBAR hits are trade-press
items and papers citing it as a data source. DALBAR's own rebuttal, appended to the Pfau
article, concedes the mechanism while disputing its relevance — *"IRR is neither necessary
nor relevant"* — which is the disagreement stated plainly. Morningstar, by contrast, does
use a proper IRR (§2.4), so Pfau's central objection does not apply to it.

**Trading-cost avoidance is real but its magnitude does not transfer.**
[Barber and Odean (2000)](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers%20current%20versions/individual_investor_performance_final.pdf)
remains the cleanest evidence that turnover, not stock selection, is what costs retail
investors: gross returns across turnover quintiles ranged only **18.5% to 18.7%** while net
returns ranged **11.4% to 18.5%**, 1991–1996, 66,465 households at one discount broker. The
whole spread is commissions and bid-ask, and *"the average round-trip trade in excess of
$1,000 costs three percent in commissions and one percent in bid-ask spread."* US retail
commissions are now zero, so the 3 pp commission component of that gap no longer exists.
The line is not booked because no current retail cost figure was verified. Note also that
the commonly quoted framing "11.4% versus the market's 17.9%" is wrong: 11.4% is the *net*
return of the highest-turnover quintile, and its *gross* return was 18.7%.

**Tax-loss harvesting: 30 bp central, and every commonly quoted version of it is
overstated.** The primary source is Chaudhuri, Burnham and Lo, *"An Empirical Evaluation
of Tax-Loss-Harvesting Alpha"*, *FAJ* 76(3), 99–108 (2020),
[accepted manuscript](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/135992) (the published text is
paywalled). Published abstract verbatim: *"Given long-term and short-term capital gains
tax rates of 15% and 35%… a tax-loss-harvesting strategy yielded a before-transaction-cost
tax alpha of 1.08% per year… When the strategy was constrained by the 'wash sale rule,'
the tax alpha decreased from 1.08% per year to 0.82% per year."* Sample: 500 largest US
stocks, July 1926 – June 2018, benchmarked against a non-harvesting buy-and-hold portfolio
of the same stocks, **after liquidation taxes** — so the "it is only deferral" objection is
already answered inside their number (1.47%/yr before liquidation taxes, 1.10% after).

Four conditions cut it hard, and all four are in the paper.

- **The base case assumes contributions of 1% of portfolio value per month**, about
  12.7%/yr of new money. For a static portfolio the full-sample figure is **0.73%**, and
  for 1995–2018 **0.39%**; for a decumulating investor in 1995–2018, **0.32%**.
- **Roughly half the alpha is rate arbitrage, not harvesting.** Verbatim: *"the annualized
  tax alpha for the entire period decreases from 1.10% to 0.51% per year when the
  long-term capital gains tax rate increases from 15% to 35%."*
- **Transaction costs take 16 bp**, leaving *"94 bps of annual alpha, net of transactions
  costs"* at 32% turnover.
- **It is unavailable through funds.** Verbatim: *"Mutual funds, however, are barred from
  passing through security-level tax losses. Similarly, index-tracking ETFs are not
  designed to harvest tax losses."* Capturing it requires direct security ownership.

The decay is the decisive counterweight, and it is now measured. Sosner, Gromis and Krasner
(AQR, *Journal of Beta Investment Strategies*, 2022 — practitioner research by a firm that
sells the product) find the active tax benefit runs *"from 155.3 bps in year 1 to 50.8 bps
in year 2 to −4.3 bps in years 10 and later"* for an investor with no new money offsetting
long-term gains, and state that *"the tax benefit is reduced to zero after approximately
five years since inception"* for that case — while the 1%/month-inflow case sustains about
80 bp indefinitely. The mechanism is ossification: harvesting systematically sells the
loss lots and retains the gain lots, so the portfolio's basis falls and the option goes out
of the money. Goldberg, Cai and Schneider,
[*Journal of Asset Management* 25(5), 445–459 (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41260-024-00374-z)
(open access; all three authors at BlackRock), report a worst-case long-only cell of
**0.19%/yr tax alpha and −0.01%/yr after-tax active return net of costs**. The budget
therefore books **0–90 bp with a central 30 bp**, conditional on a taxable account, direct
ownership, offsetting gains and continuing contributions.

**Asset location: 10 bp central, and no peer-reviewed source states a per-year figure at
all.** That absence is itself the finding. Shoven and Sialm, *Journal of Public Economics* 88(1–2), 23–38 (2004), is the cleanest
match because its benchmark is literally the uniform allocation, verbatim:
*"Optimal asset location adds an additional 6.6 percent to certainty equivalent wealth in
the base case"*, ranging 3.8–8.8%. Over their 30-year horizon that annualises to about
**21 bp/yr** — but the annualisation is *our* inference, not theirs, and a
certainty-equivalent wealth ratio is not a return.
[Dammon, Spatt and Zhang (2004, *JF* 59(3))](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2004.00655.x)
put the utility cost of the uniform policy at *"generally less than 5%"* of wealth.
Practitioner work is the only source of per-year figures and its headline is a ceiling, not
a central estimate: Vanguard's range is *"5 to 30 basis points"*, with typical cells 5–13 bp.
Two miscitations to avoid: Dammon et al.'s "65.4 basis points per year" is a **break-even
hurdle for active management** from a different model in the same paper, not a location
benefit; and Poterba, Shoven and Sialm's empirical 1962–1998 test actually **reverses** the
conventional rule for index funds, finding a high-tax investor who put an S&P 500 fund in a
tax-deferred account and municipal bonds in the taxable account ended with *"1.7 percent
less"* retirement wealth.

**Implementation and financing efficiency is not a line, and the 2026 numbers show why
there is nothing left to save.** As of 2026-08-10, Vanguard's published 30-day median
bid/ask spreads are **0.55 bp (VTI), 0.58 bp (VOO), 1.18 bp (VXUS), 2.72 bp (VB)**;
expense ratios are 3 bp; commissions are $0 at all six major US retail brokers checked;
the SEC Section 31 fee is 0.206 bp on the sell side and the FINRA TAF is immaterial. Total
round-trip friction on a broad US index ETF is about **1.3 bp**, against a 3 bp/yr holding
cost that securities lending offsets by 20–35%. There is no meaningful implementation edge
left to harvest at retail scale, and claiming one would double-count the cost line. The
figure that *is* decision-relevant is that a small-cap or international round trip
(2.7 bp, 1.2 bp) approaches a full year of expense ratio, which is the binding constraint
on rebalancing frequency — and is larger than the 2.4 bp/yr the rebalancing line earns.

**Rebalancing, net of diversification: 2.4 bp/yr central.** Derived in §1. Booked against
the stated index because a cap-weighted index of a fixed universe *is* the drifting
portfolio, subject to Pedersen's correction above. The 18 bp high figure is the 60/40
median, not the mean; the low figure is the multi-asset case where buy-and-hold captures
almost everything.

**This line has since been measured on real data and it is negative.**
[Experiment 003](rebalancing-policy.md) ran the four policies on US, developed-ex-US
and emerging equity over 420 months and found **−38.7 bp/yr on the portfolio and
−62.9 bp/yr on the US/developed-ex-US pair**, because the realised drift gap ran about
**35× `gamma_star`** and `kappa_t` trends rather than reverts. The whole of this row —
its `0` low bound included — is an **equal-drift upper bound that a real drift gap
removes**, and so, therefore, is the stated-index total below it. The numbers in this
table are left as computed rather than patched, because they are regenerated and pinned
by tests; the correction is that **24.4 bp/yr is an upper bound on the stated-index
budget, not a central estimate**, and the 0.631 probability beside it inherits that.

**Factor tilt: 21 bp central, sign not robust.** Every multiplier written out:
6.6%/yr gross long-short (the
[Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhv059) structural prior of
0.55%/month for a *true* factor at an imposed 15% volatility — in-sample, gross, before
decay) × 0.42 post-publication retention
([McLean and Pontiff 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12365)) × 0.40 long-only capture ×
0.30 portfolio exposure − 12 bp incremental fee = **21.3 bp/yr**. Halve the capture
fraction and the exposure and add trading cost and the chain goes **negative**.

Three of those four multipliers have since been measured in this repository, for value
only, and they pull in opposite directions.
[Experiment 005](factor-persistence.md#experiment-005--the-regional-replication)
pooled HML across three regions and found **+4.74 pp/yr** post-publication against
+4.56 pp/yr in its US original sample, so for that factor the retention multiplier is
nearer 1 than 0.42 — while the same experiment closed RMW and CMA on public data,
where the retention multiplier is not estimable at all
([decision 0005](../decisions/0005-factor-premia-closed-on-public-data.md)).

**The long-only capture fraction is now measured, and the measurement is that the
0.40 above is under-specified rather than wrong.**
[Experiment 007](long-only-capture.md) rebuilt HML from the six long-only portfolios
it is a difference of and priced the long leg against five defensible benchmarks. The
answers span **0.846**: the size-neutral reading, which contains book-to-market and
nothing else, is **0.520 `[0.434, 0.722]`** over 1963-07…2025-12; against the
capitalisation-weighted market the same tilt reads **0.958**, and the gap between them
is a size premium that a budget carrying a separate size line would be counting twice.
So the line above is a construction whose sign turns on a benchmark choice this page
never states. On the size-neutral capture and the US-only premium of +1.57 pp/yr the
gross factor line is **0.28–0.34 pp/yr before cost**, against 0.35–0.93 pp/yr of
assumed tilt cost — **negative**. On the pooled premium and the market-relative
capture it is positive. At 400 bp of tracking error it needs more than 500 years for
90% confidence either way. `as of 2026-08-12`.

**Securities lending: 1 bp central, computed from filings.** Net securities-lending income
as a fraction of average net assets, taken from N-CSR Statements of Operations and divided
out independently two ways: **VOO 0.07 bp** (FY2025), **IVV 0.25 bp**, **VTI 1.01 bp**,
**ITOT 1.03 bp**, **IEFA 1.11 bp** (FY2025–26). The near-identical VTI and ITOT figures
from two different sponsors cross-validate the method; the entire gap between VOO (0.07)
and VTI (1.01) is the small- and mid-cap tail. Small-cap funds earn more — VB 3.04 bp,
IWM 10.38 bp, IWC 44.8 bp — but the investor then also holds small-cap risk, so that is a
different portfolio, not a larger edge on the same one. Current contractual splits are 81%
to the fund for iShares domestic equity (stepping to 84%) and effectively ~98% for Vanguard,
**not** the 71.5–80% commonly quoted.

The decomposition that makes this predictable is
D'Avolio, "The Market for Borrowing Stock", *JFE* 66(2–3), 271–306 (2002) — paywalled at
ScienceDirect, read from a publicly hosted copy — verbatim: *"the value-weighted cost to borrow the sample loan portfolio is 25 basis points
per annum and only 7% of loan supply (by value) is borrowed"*, and *"S&P 500 constituents,
provided in excess supply by indexing lenders, are almost always general collateral"* at a
17 bp value-weighted fee. `17 bp × 7% utilisation × 80% keep rate ≈ 1 bp/yr` at the fund
level, which is exactly what the filings show. **Blocher and Whaley's much-quoted 23–28
bp is modelled *gross* revenue for 2009–2013 across 541 ETFs and is 25–100× the realised
net figure for a broad large-cap fund.** Two structural notes: the income is ordinary
income in a taxable account, and SPY, QQQ, MDY and DIA are unit investment trusts that
**cannot lend at all**.

### 2.2 What is rejected, and why

- **"Diversification return" as an edge.** It is measured against `sum_i w_i g_i`, which
  nobody can hold. Rejected as a benchmark, per Willenbrock.
- **The 1.2 pp behaviour gap.** Fulkerson et al. (2026), running Hayley's decomposition on
  Morningstar's 2015–2024 sample, put the timing component at **0.10 pp/yr** of a 0.17 pp
  gap, and show separately that an end-of-month asset-weighting convention accounted for
  more than a percentage point of the historical figure. Rejected at its headline
  magnitude, retained at 15 bp central.
- **"Not trading" as a quantified edge, and as a line in the contractual budget.**
  Rejected on both counts (§2.4). The gap is identically zero for an investor with no cash
  flows, Morningstar disclaims the individual-level reading of it, and it is measured
  against a benchmark the budget may not aggregate with. The application may display the
  15 bp line against the average investor; it may never add it to the 109 bp.
- **The index-inclusion cost as a current benchmark-choice saving.** Real at 21–28 bp for
  1990–2005; approximately zero by the 2010s on two independent measurements. Rejected as
  a 2026 magnitude.
- **Barber–Odean's 6.5 pp turnover penalty as a current number.** Its commission
  component no longer exists. Rejected as a 2026 magnitude; retained as a mechanism.
- **Implementation and financing efficiency.** The framework is explicit that this is not
  alpha. Avoiding a bad spread or a wide borrow is a cost reduction already counted in the
  cost line, and counting it again is a double count.
- **Distinct risk premia (term, credit, insurance, illiquidity).** These are real
  compensation for real risk, but they are a *different benchmark*, not outperformance of
  an equity index. Booking a term premium as an "edge" over an equity index is a
  benchmark switch, not a return source. Excluded by construction.
- **Combining several 55%-win-rate strategies.** The framework already shows twenty
  independent 55% bets give a strict majority only 59.1% of the time, and that
  independence is itself estimated from the same selected history.

### 2.3 The double counts this budget avoids

- **Fees and timing do not overlap.** Morningstar's investor return is already net of
  fund expenses, so the fee saving and the timing saving measure different dollars.
- **The behaviour gap and the contractual budget cannot be added at all**, being measured
  against different benchmarks. §2.4 gives the argument and corrects four pages that
  described *not trading* as part of the 109 bp.
- **Trading-cost avoidance and the behaviour gap would overlap**, because Barber–Odean's
  penalty *is* transaction costs. Only one is booked, and it is the smaller one.
- **Implementation efficiency and the cost line would overlap.** A tighter spread or a
  cheaper share class is a cost reduction already counted; booking it twice is the most
  common inflation in this kind of budget.
- **Tax-loss harvesting and asset location are separable**, one being the realisation of
  losses within an account and the other the choice of which account holds what. But both
  are unavailable to the same investor for the same reason — a single tax-deferred account
  with no taxable holdings zeroes both at once, so their *conditions* are correlated even
  though their mechanisms are not.
- **Securities lending and the cost line are separable** (one is a fund revenue, the other
  a fund charge), though a fund that lends aggressively and rebates little is charging a
  hidden fee and must be assessed on tracking difference, not on the two lines separately.
- **Rebalancing and the factor tilt sit inside the same equity portfolio** and share its
  beta and its crises, so combining their tracking errors in quadrature assumes an
  **independence that is optimistic**. The stated-index total's 401 bp is therefore a
  lower bound on its own dispersion.

### 2.4 The behaviour gap is a different benchmark, not a missing line

**Question.** The gap between the return a fund earns and the return its investors earn
is the most direct measurement of "beat the portfolio you would otherwise have owned",
which is this page's central claim. It is booked at 15 bp above and it is absent from the
[~109 bp contractual budget](structural-and-tax-edges.md#the-ledger). Should it join
that budget, and may the application present *not trading* as a quantified edge?

**No, and no. It cannot join, for a reason that is structural rather than empirical.**
The gap is measured against **the average investor in the same fund**; the 109 bp budget
is measured against **the same investor's own plausible alternative product and account
choice**. Those are two of the three benchmarks in
[`studies/outperformance_horizon.py`](../../research/src/portfolio_edge/studies/outperformance_horizon.py),
and `aggregate()` **raises** rather than summing across them, verbatim: *"Raises if the
components do not share a benchmark, because summing across benchmarks is the
double-counting error this module is built to prevent."* The `Benchmark` enum says the
same thing at the point of definition — *"These never aggregate across classes."* So the
answer is already enforced in code, and the only thing missing was saying it in prose.

**A dollar-weighted gap is undefined for the investor this repository describes.** The
gap is the difference between a fund's time-weighted return and the internal rate of
return on its investors' dated cash flows. With no cash flows there is no difference:
for a lump sum held throughout, the IRR **is** the geometric return, so the gap is
exactly zero — not small, not noisy, identically zero. An investor who buys a broad
index fund and does not trade has nothing left to collect here, because the quantity
being measured does not exist for them. **"Not trading" cannot be a line in a budget
whose own arithmetic sets it to zero.**

**And for a saver who does have cash flows, the sign is set by the market, not by the
saver.** Take an investor making level contributions with no timing intent whatsoever.
On a two-year path of `+50%, −20%` the time-weighted return is 9.5445%/yr and the
dollar-weighted return is 0.0000%/yr — a gap of **−9.54 pp/yr**. Reverse the two
returns, leaving the time-weighted return identical, and the same investor posts
**+12.21 pp/yr**. Across all 24 orderings of a four-year path with a fixed time-weighted
return, the gap spans **−8.66 to +9.86 pp** and is negative 45.8% of the time. Under
20,000 simulated ten-year paths with level monthly contributions and iid lognormal
returns at 8%/15%, the gap has **mean +0.15 pp, standard deviation 2.60 pp**, and is
below −1.2 pp about **30%** of the time.

Two consequences, and the second is the one that matters.

- **The mechanical component is mean-zero and enormous.** It does not create a
  systematic drag, so a persistently negative population gap needs a cause the mechanics
  alone do not supply. §2.1 has the two candidates already sourced, and neither is
  restraint: Fulkerson et al. put fund-investor timing at **0.10%/yr**, and Hayley
  decomposes Dichev's 1.3 pp gap into **−0.21 pp of timing and −0.95 pp of bias**.
- **A single measured gap carries 2.6 pp of mechanical noise, so it cannot identify an
  individual.** No investor can be told from this statistic whether they personally
  mistimed. The 15 bp line is therefore a **population** claim about the average
  investor, and it stays exactly where it is: probabilistic, 150 bp of tracking error,
  `AVERAGE_INVESTOR`, never added to the 109 bp.

**Morningstar says all of this itself, and the sentences are easy to miss.** *Mind the
Gap 2026* (Jeffrey Ptak, published 2026-08-06; PDF retrieved and read in full
2026-08-12) pools roughly **23,000** US open-end funds and ETFs and computes *"the
constant monthly rate of return that makes the pool's beginning net assets equal its
ending assets with all monthly cash flows accounted for"* — a proper IRR, and the reason
Pfau's objection to DALBAR does not transfer. But the thing it is compared against is a
**lump sum**: the 9.9% total return is stated *"assuming an initial lump-sum purchase"*.
So the headline 1.2 pp is the difference between the average dated dollar and a dollar
invested on 2016-01-01 — exactly the pairing the arithmetic above shows to be
path-determined. Three further caveats, verbatim, and each is load-bearing:

- *"It is important to note that this estimate is not a proxy for the average investor's
  dollar-weighted return, as this will depend on the particular magnitude and timing of
  an individual investor's purchases and sales, which is beyond the scope of this
  study."* **The vendor disclaims the individual-level reading outright.**
- *"it is a truism that for the theoretical 'total market,' there can be no 'gap,' as for
  every buyer, there is a seller, meaning one's shortfall is another's surplus."* The gap
  is **zero-sum**, so it cannot be a population-wide edge — only a redistribution between
  investors. That is precisely why it belongs to `AVERAGE_INVESTOR` and nowhere else.
  **Morningstar qualifies this in the next paragraph and the qualification has to travel
  with the quote:** the study covers only US open-end funds and ETFs that existed at
  2016-01-01, which is *"only a subset of the global market"*, so *"the zero-sum
  total-market truism won't necessarily hold for it as theory might suggest."* The
  argument above therefore rests on the truism holding for the **total** market, which is
  an identity, and not on Morningstar's sample reproducing it, which is not measured.
- *"Nor should timing effects be interpreted as literal opportunity costs. For instance,
  the investor who bought a fund after it had risen sharply, for reasons or circumstances
  outside their control, hasn't 'lost' out on those earlier gains."*

One methodological limit Morningstar states and nobody quotes: *"we do not correct for
creation bias… we exclude funds that launched after Dec. 31, 2015, from the study."*

**The dispersion is the actionable finding, not the headline.** Exhibit 4, gap in

**What is genuinely controllable is product selection, and it is measured.** The gap runs
from **−0.4 pp** on the least-volatile quintile to **−2.1 pp** on the most volatile, and

equity index fund (**−0.1 pp**) and the alternatives shelf is roughly **1.5 pp/yr** —
larger than the entire 109 bp contractual budget. So there is a real, large, controllable
quantity here, and the action that captures it is **choosing broad, low-volatility,
low-tracking-error funds** rather than exercising restraint inside a volatile one.

**Which is largely what the [recommended portfolio](portfolio-recommendation.md) already
does.** Its US sleeve sits in the least-volatile, lowest-tracking-error cells of
Morningstar's own cross-section, where the measured gap is 0.0 to −0.4 pp. An investor
who holds that construction has **already spent** most of this edge; the residual on the
US side is the 0 to 10 bp between large blend and a US equity index fund, not 120 bp.
Booking it on top of the 109 bp would be the same decision counted twice — the fund
choice is *already* the 49 bp cost line — which is precisely what §2.3 exists to prevent.

goes away"* is false as stated, and no line on this page may rest on it.
`as of 2026-08-12`, from Exhibit 8, ten years ended 2025-12-31.

**The counter-case is stronger than its citation record suggests, and it is three
independent attacks.** Keswani and Stolin, *Finance Research Letters* 5(4) (2008),
abstract verbatim: *"Dichev's findings are not robust."* Their subperiod split of the
NYSE/AMEX gap is **+1.8 pp for 1926–1951, −0.2 pp for 1951–1977 and −0.3 pp for
1977–2002** — the effect is confined to the pre-war data and reverses afterwards — and
replacing Datastream with a comprehensive price database moves the UK gap *"from 1.1 to
−1.3 percent"*. Johnston, Hatem and Paul, *Quarterly Journal of Finance and Accounting*
53(1/2) (2015), abstract only, make the purely arithmetic claim: *"the relative magnitude
of beginning and ending balances are the principal determinants of the differences in the
estimated returns"* — the gap tracks the asset base, which is what §2.4's simulation
shows. And Hayley attacks **his own 2014 method** in a 2019 working paper
([free full text](https://core.ac.uk/download/389055452.pdf)): using in-sample mean
returns means *"above-average returns prior to any given period must by construction be
followed by subsequent below-average returns… This is an entirely spurious correlation."*
His simulations put roughly **a third of the remaining measured gap** in that category.
Stack the three and very little survives.

**At the individual level the quantity has never been measured at all, and that is the
finding.** Every dollar-weighted gap in this literature — Dichev, Friesen–Sapp,
Dichev–Yu, Morningstar, DALBAR — is computed on **fund-level or market-level aggregate
flow series**. No peer-reviewed study computes a dollar-weighted-versus-time-weighted gap
for identifiable individuals. What the account-level evidence does show cuts against the
folklore twice over. Barber and Odean's 66,465 households earned an average **gross 18.7%
against the market's 17.9%** — they beat the index before costs — and their 1.5 pp net
shortfall is transaction costs, not timing; in the Taiwan whole-market study the authors
relegate *"a somewhat smaller role… to poor market timing choices"* than to selection,
commissions or transaction tax. And Calvet, Campbell and Sodini's complete Swedish
administrative panel of ~4.8m households finds a median return loss of **−0.11%/yr
against the domestic index** — the median household *beats* it — while their 2009
companion finds households offset *"about one half of idiosyncratic passive variations in
the risky asset share"*, which is contrarian rebalancing, not return chasing. **The modal
retail investor in complete population data is not the investor the behaviour-gap
narrative describes.**

**Correction this makes to material already in the repository.** Four places describe
*not trading* as part of the ~109 bp contractual budget: the
[framework](portfolio-edge-research-framework.md) §1 and its design-map row, the
[recommendation page](portfolio-recommendation.md) §5.4, and
[decisions 0006](../decisions/0006-reference-portfolio-without-promotion.md) and
[0007](../decisions/0007-application-may-render-research.md). The ledger that produces
109 bp is `49 + 30 + 10 + 23 + 5 − 3.4 − 4.4` and contains no behaviour line at all. The
client's `src/content/edgeBudget.ts` already gets this right and carries no such line;
the prose had drifted from the ledger. All four are corrected in this change.
`as of 2026-08-12`.

**Status: `exploratory`, and the ceiling is low by construction.** This is a literature
and benchmark-consistency synthesis, not an experiment. The arithmetic above is a
closed-form and Monte Carlo demonstration on synthetic paths with no market data; it
bounds what the statistic *can* mean, and it cannot measure what any real population of
investors did.

## 3. What probability is actually attainable

For a relative log return with edge `e` and tracking error `s`, both annual and
independent, `P(outperform) = Phi(e sqrt(T) / s)` and `T(confidence) = (z s / e)**2`. The
horizon scales with the **square** of `s/e`.

| Edge, against 400 bp tracking error | 10 yr | 20 yr | 30 yr | 50 yr | 90% at | 95% at |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 bp | 0.532 | 0.545 | 0.554 | 0.570 | 2,628 yr | 4,329 yr |
| 25 bp | 0.578 | 0.610 | 0.634 | 0.671 | 420 yr | 693 yr |
| **50 bp** | **0.654** | **0.712** | **0.753** | **0.812** | **105 yr** | **173 yr** |
| 100 bp | 0.785 | 0.868 | 0.915 | 0.961 | 26 yr | 43 yr |
| 200 bp | 0.943 | 0.987 | 0.997 | 1.000 | 7 yr | 11 yr |

The same 50 bp edge, against different tracking errors, reaching 90%: **24 days** at 10 bp,
1.6 years at 50 bp, 6.6 years at 100 bp, 26 years at 200 bp, **105 years** at 400 bp.
Tracking error, not edge size, decides whether a lifetime is enough.

Read the other way: against 400 bp of tracking error, thirty years can only *demonstrate*
an edge of 94 bp/yr at 90% confidence, and fifty years 72 bp. **No probabilistic line in
this budget is demonstrable from the investor's own experience.** Evidence has to come from
somewhere other than a track record.

**The attainable answer, by benchmark.**

| Benchmark | Central edge | TE | 10 yr | 20 yr | 30 yr | 50 yr | 90% at |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stated index | 24.4 bp | 401 bp | 0.576 | 0.607 | **0.631** | 0.667 | ~443 yr |
| Average investor | 15 bp | 150 bp | 0.624 | 0.673 | **0.708** | 0.760 | ~164 yr |
| Own counterfactual | 89 bp | 41 bp | ~1.00 | ~1.00 | **~1.00** | ~1.00 | ~4 months |

That is the whole answer to "can you near-definitively beat the market". You can
near-definitively beat *the portfolio you would otherwise have owned*, by roughly 90 basis
points a year, and reach 99% confidence in about fourteen months, because most of that edge
is contractual rather than statistical. You cannot near-definitively beat the index, at any
horizon a human has: the honest number there is a **63% chance of being ahead after thirty
years**, and no amount of waiting converts it into certainty, because the edge and its
noise both scale in the way that keeps them in the same ratio.

The asymmetry between the last two rows is the entire practical finding, and it is not
about markets at all. It is about which benchmark a saving is measured against.

## 4. Assumptions, open questions, and provenance

**Assumptions, stated so they can be attacked.** Returns are lognormal with constant
parameters and no jumps; there are no taxes, no trading costs and no cash flows in §1;
budget components' relative returns are mutually independent; the edge in §3 is *known*
rather than estimated, which removes the dominant source of uncertainty and makes every
probability an upper bound. Every one of these fails in the direction that reduces the
measured advantage.

**Open questions this page does not settle.**

1. **Which benchmark the factor line's capture term may be taken against.** The
   capture fraction itself is no longer a gap: [Experiment 007](long-only-capture.md)
   measured it at 0.520 `[0.434, 0.722]` size-neutral and 0.958 against the market,
   a 0.846 range across five defensible benchmarks. What remains open is which of
   those a budget with no separate size line is entitled to book, and the sign of the
   factor line still depends on the answer.
2. **Tax outside the US, and the step-up interaction.** Every tax figure here is US and
   jurisdiction-specific. The harvesting alpha sits between the after-liquidation 1.10% and
   the before-liquidation 1.47% depending on whether the low-basis position is ever sold —
   which turns on 26 U.S.C. §1014 basis step-up at death and on charitable donation, both
   outside every model cited. Tax law must be a dated, versioned input, never a constant.
3. **Tracking difference and after-tax return of actual funds.** Neither ICI nor
   Morningstar publishes an all-in figure, and none was retrievable, so the 3 bp expense
   ratio is a floor on the true holding cost rather than the whole of it.
4. **Whether equal weighting inside equities is a size tilt or a harvest.** §1 shows the
   harvest is worth about 4 bp/yr at 30 years, so essentially all of any observed
   equal-weight advantage must be a factor tilt. Testing that requires the framework's
   factor protocol, not this page.
5. **Whether the components' relative returns are independent.** The quadrature rule in
   the budget assumes it; a factor tilt and a rebalancing policy on the same equity
   portfolio plainly are not. Correlated components would widen the stated-index
   dispersion and lower the 0.631.
6. **Whether the 15 bp central estimate should now move, given the dispersion.** The
   category and volatility breakdowns in §2.4 were retrieved and closed the question they
   were asked to answer, but they also make the 5–60 bp range look poorly shaped rather
   than merely wide. The measured gap for the products this repository recommends is
   **0.0 to −0.4 pp**, which argues the *low* end should be near zero, while the gap for
   the products it rejects runs to **−13.2 pp**, which argues the high end is far above
   60 bp for an investor who would genuinely have bought them — Hsu, Myers and Whitby's
   131–401 bp by style and fee quintile point the same way. The line is probably two
   different quantities — a residual near zero for the recommended construction, and a
   large product-selection saving that the 49 bp cost line already partly claims. Splitting
   it requires deciding how much of the volatility effect is the fund choice the cost line
   is already paid for, and that was not settled here.

**Reproducibility.** All figures regenerate from
`research/src/portfolio_edge/studies/` with `uv run pytest tests/unit/test_studies_*.py`.
Simulations use `numpy.random.default_rng` with the committed seed `20260812`; closed forms
use no randomness at all. Retrieval date for every source above: **2026-08-12**.

**One exception, stated so it is not mistaken for a pinned figure.** The dollar-weighted
arithmetic in §2.4 is **not yet in the `studies` package and not pinned by a test**. It
is reproducible from its stated parameters — level contributions at the start of each
period; IRR as the largest positive real root of `sum_t cf_t x**(n−t) = 0` with
`x = 1 + i`; for the Monte Carlo, 20,000 paths of 120 monthly iid lognormal returns at
`mu = 8%/yr`, `sigma = 15%/yr`, `numpy.random.default_rng(20260812)` — but until it has
a module and a test it is weaker evidence than everything else on this page, and it
should acquire both before any feature leans on it.

## Consequence for this repository

1. **Declare the objective.** Net geometric growth — equivalently expected log wealth — with
   a stated drawdown constraint, recorded as a *preference* justified by Breiman's
   asymptotic theorem, not as a proof that expected-terminal-wealth investors are wrong.
   This closes the framework's open question 2.
2. **Every result must carry a benchmark and a certainty class.** The evidence card in the
   product contract needs two more fields: which of the three benchmarks, and deterministic
   or probabilistic. A number without both is not reportable.
3. **Report probability of outperformance by horizon, never a point estimate of edge.**
   The horizon table in §3 is the shape every result should take. A 24 bp central edge with
   a 0.63 thirty-year probability is an honest output; "+24 bp/yr" is not.
4. **Rank features by their effect on the budget.** Cost, tax and account-type selection is
   worth about 89 deterministic basis points. Rebalancing policy is worth about 2.4
   probabilistic basis points — less than the round-trip spread on a small-cap ETF. The
   optimiser is the least valuable part of the application, and building
   it first would be building the smallest line in the budget.
5. **Do not build a rebalancing-bonus feature.** The framework already forbids the copy;
   this page supplies the magnitude. A tool that shows a user their expected rebalancing
   gain must show 2.4 bp/yr against a 5th percentile of −80 bp/yr, or it is misleading.
6. **The `studies` package is the executable record.** Any change to a number on this page
   changes a test, by construction.
7. **Do not build a behaviour-gap feature, and do not let "not trading" into the 109 bp.**
   The application may render the 15 bp line, labelled `AVERAGE_INVESTOR` and
   `probabilistic`, and it must never sum it with the contractual budget — which is
   already [decision 0007](../decisions/0007-application-may-render-research.md)
   constraint 3 and is enforced by `aggregate()`. It must not compute a personal behaviour
   gap for a reader: the source disclaims that reading, the statistic carries about 2.6 pp
   of mechanical noise, and no study has ever measured it for an identifiable individual.
   What the application *may* honestly say is the product-selection finding — the measured
   gap runs from 0.0 pp on a broad large-blend fund to −13.2 pp on the most volatile
   alternatives — because that is a fund choice, computable from the reader's own holdings.
8. **Pin §2.4's arithmetic before anything leans on it.** It wants a small
   `studies/investor_return_gap.py` and a test, on the same footing as every other number
   here. Until then it is the one unpinned block on the page.
