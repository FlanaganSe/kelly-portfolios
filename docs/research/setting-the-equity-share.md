# Setting the equity share: what is arithmetic, what is preference, and what neither can reach

**Question.** The repository has declared net geometric growth as its objective and has
refused to set the equity/bond split. Given both of those, what can actually be said
about setting it?

**Decision it informs.** What a disciplined answer to the largest decision in the
portfolio looks like, and which parts of it an application may compute rather than
ask. It does **not** set the split, and the refusal in
[the recommendation](portfolio-recommendation.md) §1.1 stands.

**Out of scope.** A forecast of any market. A recommended number. Glide paths as
products. Annuities, which change the problem rather than the parameter.

`as of 2026-08-12`. Every measured figure below is regenerated by
[`studies/equity_share.py`](../../research/src/portfolio_edge/studies/equity_share.py)
and pinned in `research/tests/unit/test_studies_equity_share.py`.

---

## Conclusion, stated directly

1. **The objective does not choose the split. The constraint does.** The declared
   objective is net geometric growth *subject to a drawdown or expected-shortfall
   constraint* ([framework](portfolio-edge-research-framework.md), assumptions), and
   growth-optimal sizing contains no risk aversion parameter at all. Under the
   repository's own zero-leverage rule the growth objective on its own returns a
   corner solution — **100% equity**, for any equity premium over bonds above about
   **2.2%/yr arithmetic**. Every bond in variants B and C is there because of the
   constraint, not the objective, and the constraint is a single number nobody has
   supplied.
2. **Because the constraint binds, the only available error is underbetting, and
   underbetting is cheap.** Growth at exposure `f` times the growth-optimal exposure
   retains **`1 − (1 − f)²`** of the peak excess growth — a parameter-free expression.
   Half the growth-optimal exposure keeps **75%**. Twice it keeps **0%**. With
   leverage at zero and the unconstrained optimum above 1.0, every admissible
   portfolio sits on the gentle left branch of that parabola. That is the strongest
   thing this page has to say to a nervous reader.
3. **The equity share is worth about as much per year as the entire measured edge
   budget, and takes about twenty-five years to prove rather than twelve months.**
   Moving 60/40 to 90/10 was worth **+127 bp/yr** against **485 bp/yr** of tracking
   error on 1963-07…2025-12 — a 0.924 chance of being ahead after thirty years and 90%
   confidence at **24 years**. The ~109 bp contractual budget reaches 99% in about
   twelve months against 46 bp. Same size, wholly different certainty class, and
   [they may never be added](expected-edge-decomposition.md) because they carry
   different benchmarks.
4. **The case for fractional Kelly is variance, not bias, and it is weaker than it is
   usually made.** The expected annual growth given up by using an estimated optimum is
   exactly **`1/(2T)`** — 0.80%/yr on a 62-year sample, 2.50%/yr on a 20-year one, free
   of every other parameter. The growth-maximising shrinkage is
   **`f* = S²T/(S²T + 1)`**, which on this repository's own US equity sample is
   **0.931**, not 0.5. Half Kelly is the claim that 62 years of record are worth
   **4.7 years** of stationary information. That may be a good claim. It is a claim
   about non-stationarity, and it should be argued as one.
5. **Sequence risk is exactly what §1.1 says it is, and it has a sign.** Across 20,000
   reorderings of one fixed 360-month return record, a lump sum's terminal wealth was
   **identical to floating-point precision**. A contributor's spanned **2.18×** between
   the 5th and 95th percentiles and a withdrawer's **1.77×**, and the correlation with
   first-decade returns was **−0.775 for the contributor and +0.775 for the
   withdrawer** — the same number with opposite signs. The split depends on the shape
   of cash flows, not on horizon.
6. **Variant C bundles two situations with opposite answers.** In real terms over a
   30-year retirement, a 20% equity portfolio drawing 4%/yr real failed in **6.82%** of
   reorderings against **2.43%** at 60% equity; at a 5% draw, failure fell
   *monotonically* from 47.34% at 20% equity to 12.86% at 90%. A short horizon argues
   for fewer equities. A long drawdown at a demanding withdrawal rate argues for more.
   §1.1's variant C row currently reads them as one case.
7. **The bond side is a risk brake whose braking is regime-dependent, and that is a
   weaker claim than the usual one.** On this repository's own data with a modelled
   ten-year bond, the equity/bond beta ran **+0.129 over 1974-07…1999-12, −0.055 over
   2000-01…2022-09, +0.116 over 2022-10…2024-06 and −0.109 over the eighteen months
   since**. The all-bond portfolio drew down **−25.1%**, deeper than the 30/70 mix's
   **−17.9%**. Bonds are not a floor.
8. **Status.** Sections 1, 2 and 3 below are **arithmetic**: closed forms, exact given
   their inputs, and not evidence about any market. Sections 5, 6 and 7 are
   **`exploratory`** at best — one sample, one country, one modelled bond series, and
   in the retirement case a permutation null that deliberately destroys serial
   dependence. Nothing here is promoted and nothing here reopens
   [decision 0004](../decisions/0004-no-sleeve-promoted.md).

## 1. The part that is arithmetic

### 1.1 The growth parabola, and why the cost of being wrong is small on one side

[`core/kelly.py`](../../research/src/portfolio_edge/core/kelly.py) already carries the
algebra. Written about its vertex,

```
g(L) = r + 0.5 sigma**2 [ (L*)**2 − (L − L*)**2 ],    L* = (mu − r) / sigma**2
```

Divide the excess of `g` over the cash rate by its peak and every parameter cancels.
At exposure `L = f L*`:

```
growth retained  =  1 − (1 − f)**2   of the peak excess growth
```

| `f` (fraction of the growth-optimal exposure) | Peak excess growth retained |
| ---: | ---: |
| 0.25 | 0.438 |
| **0.50** | **0.750** |
| 0.75 | 0.938 |
| **1.00** | **1.000** |
| 1.50 | 0.750 |
| **2.00** | **0.000** — growth falls back to cash |
| 3.00 | **−3.000** — three times the peak, given away |

The parabola is symmetric in `L`, so the asymmetry people mean is *multiplicative*.
Being at half the growth-optimal exposure and being at twice it are both a factor of
two away. One costs a quarter of the peak excess growth; the other costs all of it,
and carries four times the variance while doing so. That is why
[MacLean, Thorp and Ziemba (2010)](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/157/Papers/Good_Bad_Kelly.pdf)
write, of the same result, that *"it never pays to bet more than the Kelly strategy
because then risk increases (lower security) and growth decreases, so Kelly dominates
all these strategies in geometric risk-return or mean-variance space"* (retrieved
2026-08-12).

The consequence for this repository is specific.
[Decision 0004](../decisions/0004-no-sleeve-promoted.md) sets leverage to zero, and
§1.2 shows the unconstrained optimum sits well above 1.0. So the whole feasible range
is on the left branch. **With leverage at zero you cannot overbet the equity/bond
decision.** The expensive half of the curve is not reachable.

### 1.2 Where the optimum is, and why the constraint binds

For a fully invested, long-only two-asset mix rebalanced continuously, maximising the
growth rate gives

```
w* = ( mu_e − mu_b + sigma_b**2 − rho sigma_e sigma_b )
     / ( sigma_e**2 + sigma_b**2 − 2 rho sigma_e sigma_b )
```

Both `mu` terms are forecasts. So are all three second moments. **This step cannot be
run without a forecast, and the repository does not make one**
([recommendation](portfolio-recommendation.md) §7).

The honest direction to read it in is backwards. Invert it, and a chosen equity share
becomes the forecast it always was. At the sample second moments of this repository's
own US series — `sigma_e` 15.40%, `sigma_b` 6.73% for the modelled ten-year bond — the
arithmetic equity-over-bond premium at which each share is growth-optimal is:

| `rho` | w = 0.4 | w = 0.6 | w = 0.8 | **w = 1.0** |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| −0.30 | 0.61% | 1.30% | 1.99% | **2.68%** |
| 0.00 | 0.68% | 1.24% | 1.81% | **2.37%** |
| +0.133 (sample) | 0.70% | 1.21% | 1.72% | **2.23%** |
| +0.30 | 0.74% | 1.18% | 1.62% | **2.06%** |

Read the last column. **A 100% equity portfolio is growth-optimal for any expected
equity-over-bond premium above about 2.1 to 2.7%/yr arithmetic.** Read the middle
column: choosing 60/40 asserts that equities will beat bonds by about **1.2%/yr**, and
no more. Anyone who would not write that forecast down should notice that holding
60/40 writes it down for them.

For scale, and as an illustration rather than a forecast: over 1963-07…2025-12 the
realised arithmetic premium of US equity over the modelled ten-year bond was
**5.51%/yr**, at which the unconstrained `w*` is **2.28**. Against cash the same
statement is simpler still — `L* = 1` requires only `mu − r = sigma**2`, which at
15.40% volatility is **2.37%/yr**.

**This is the finding that most needs stating plainly.** Growth-optimal sizing, under
the repository's own zero-leverage rule, does not produce a balanced portfolio. It
produces a corner. Every bond in variants B and C is bought by something the objective
does not contain. Sections 2 to 4 are the candidates for what that something is, and
only one of them survives well.

### 1.3 What one step up the ladder is actually worth

Measured on the same window, constant mix rebalanced monthly, log tracking error
against the lower rung:

| Move | Edge | Tracking error | P(ahead, 30 yr) | 90% confident at |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 60/40 → 70/30 | +45.1 bp/yr | 161 bp/yr | 0.937 | 21 yr |
| 60/40 → 80/20 | +87.5 bp/yr | 323 bp/yr | 0.931 | 22 yr |
| **60/40 → 90/10** | **+127.1 bp/yr** | **485 bp/yr** | **0.924** | **24 yr** |
| 60/40 → 100/0 | +163.9 bp/yr | 648 bp/yr | 0.917 | 26 yr |
| 40/60 → 100/0 | +261.9 bp/yr | 968 bp/yr | 0.931 | 22 yr |

Two things to take from it. First, the information ratio is nearly constant up the
ladder, because edge and tracking error both scale with the weight difference — so the
*size* of the step barely changes how long the decision takes to prove. It is about
twenty-five years either way.

Second, the comparison with the rest of the repository. The contractual budget is
**~109 bp/yr against 46 bp** of tracking error and is 99% settled in about twelve
months ([structural and tax-aware edges](structural-and-tax-edges.md)). The 60/40 →
90/10 move is **+127 bp/yr against 485 bp**. Those are the same order of magnitude in
expected return and more than twenty times apart in how fast you find out. The often-repeated
claim that the equity share dwarfs everything else is right about the **risk** — 15
points of maximum drawdown against an edge budget that has no drawdown term at all —
and roughly wrong about the **return**. Both figures are historical, and the second
must never be added to the first: they are measured against different benchmarks and
[`studies/outperformance_horizon.py`](../../research/src/portfolio_edge/studies/outperformance_horizon.py)
raises on the attempt.

## 2. Why full Kelly is not the answer even on its own terms

Breiman's theorem is asymptotic. Its own statement says so:
*"asymptotically, S\*ₙ is as large as the Sₙ provided by any strategy Λ"*
([Breiman 1961](https://projecteuclid.org/ebook/Download?urlId=bsmsp%2F1200512159&isFullBook=false),
§3, quoted in the [edge decomposition](expected-edge-decomposition.md) §1.3).
Asymptotically is not a horizon. Samuelson's objection at finite horizons stands, and
that page already concedes it.

Three separate arguments get made for cutting the fraction. They are not equally good.

### 2.1 The estimation-error argument, done exactly

Take `sigma` known and `muhat ~ N(mu, sigma²/T)` over `T` years. Then

```
SE( Lhat* ) = 1 / ( sigma sqrt(T) )
E[ g(Lhat*) ] = g(L*) − 0.5 sigma**2 Var(Lhat*) = g(L*) − 1 / (2 T)
```

The second line is exact and is the sharpest thing on this page. **The expected annual
growth given up by estimating the growth-optimal exposure is `1/(2T)`, free of `mu`,
`sigma` and `r`.** It is a pure consequence of the objective being quadratic in the
exposure error. Verified against a 400,000-draw seeded simulation in
`test_plug_in_growth_cost_and_shrinkage_against_monte_carlo`.

Shrink the plug-in by `f` and minimise the same expected shortfall — squared bias
`(1 − f)² L*²` against variance `f² Var(Lhat*)` — and the optimum is

```
f* = S**2 T / ( S**2 T + 1 ),      S = Sharpe ratio
```

It depends on the data only through `S²T`, which is the sample's whole information
content about the mean. On this repository's US market series over 1963-07…2025-12,
`S = 0.4631` against cash:

| Sample length | `SE(Lhat*)` | Growth cost `1/(2T)` | `f*` at S = 0.4631 |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 10 yr | 2.05 | **5.00%/yr** | 0.682 |
| 20 yr | 1.45 | **2.50%/yr** | 0.811 |
| 30 yr | 1.19 | **1.67%/yr** | 0.866 |
| **62.5 yr** (the whole sample) | 0.82 | **0.80%/yr** | **0.931** |

Two readings, and both matter.

**The cost of not knowing the mean is large.** At 0.80%/yr over the longest sample
anyone has, it is about three-quarters of the whole 109 bp contractual budget; at a
twenty-year sample it is 2.50%/yr, more than twice it. This is the
quantitative form of the point
[Merton (1980)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(80)90007-0) made: the precision of a
mean estimate improves with the *calendar span* of the sample and not with sampling
more finely inside it, so it improves slowly and there is no way to buy your way out.
It is also why Chopra and Ziemba (1993), *Journal of Portfolio Management* 19(2):6–11,
find errors in means about **eleven times** as damaging as errors in variances and
**twenty-two times** as damaging as errors in covariances at a risk tolerance of 50 —
the widely quoted 20:2:1 ratio — and worse still as risk aversion falls, a log
investor's `R_A(w) = 1/w` being about as low as it gets. Their table is reproduced in
MacLean, Thorp and Ziemba (2010); the original is paywalled and **was not read here**.

**And the honest shrinkage is 0.93, not 0.5.** This is where the usual telling goes
wrong, and it is worth being precise, because the brief this page answers put it the
common way. With `sigma` known, the plug-in optimum is *not* biased upward — it is
unbiased and noisy, and what the noise damages is the achieved growth, not the
estimate. Estimating `sigma` too *does* bias it upward, by `(n−1)/(n−3)`, which on 750
monthly observations is **1.00268**: 0.27%, a rounding error. Selection across many
candidate assets biases the winner's estimate upward properly, but there is no
selection in an equity/bond split — there are two assets and both are held.

So the estimation-error argument, run correctly on this sample, supports a fraction
around 0.9. Invert the formula and the folk rule becomes legible:
`T = f / ((1 − f) S²)`, so **half Kelly on a 0.4631-Sharpe asset is the assertion that
the entire 62-year record is worth 4.66 years of stationary information.** That is a
defensible claim — regimes change, the bond-stock sign flip in §6 is direct evidence
for it — but it is a claim about non-stationarity, and it should be defended as one
rather than smuggled in as a statistical correction.

### 2.2 The other two arguments

**Variance of the growth rate.** Full Kelly's wealth path is violent. MacLean, Thorp
and Ziemba put it plainly: *"The main disadvantage of the Kelly criterion is that its
suggested wagers may be very large. Hence, the Kelly criterion can be very risky in the
short term."* Their own reading of two long-horizon investors is that Buffett behaved
*"similar to a fully Kelly bettor (subject to the constraint of no borrowing)"* and
Keynes *"like a 80% Kelly bettor"*. Note the parenthesis: it is the same constraint
this repository imposes, and under it full Kelly on equities is simply 100% equity.
[MacLean, Ziemba and Blazenko (1992)](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.38.11.1562),
*Management Science* 38(11):1562–1585, is the formal treatment of the growth-security
trade-off; it does not name a fraction and neither does anything else here.

**Risk-constrained Kelly.** Framework open decision 8 already asks whether it beats
fractional Kelly, notes Busseti, Ryu and Boyd's ~34% growth advantage at matched
drawdown risk on a finite-outcome case, and notes that the advantage vanished on that
paper's own fat-tailed mixture. It remains untested here and it remains correctly
deferred. It sizes an edge; there is no edge.

**What the argument actually supports.** A fraction between about 0.5 and 0.9 of the
growth-optimal exposure, with the low end justified by non-stationarity rather than by
sampling error, and the high end by the arithmetic above. On the numbers in §1.2 that
range maps to an equity share of roughly **1.1 to 2.1 times** a fully invested
portfolio — which is to say the no-leverage constraint still binds across the whole of
it. **Fractional Kelly does not produce a bond allocation either.** It changes which
side of the binding constraint you are standing on, and nothing else.

## 3. Sequence risk, verified and given a sign

[The recommendation](portfolio-recommendation.md) §1.1 asserts that without external
cash flows, permuting returns leaves terminal wealth unchanged, so sequence risk is a
cash-flow interaction and not a premium. **That is exactly right.** Terminal wealth is
`W0 · prod(1 + r_t)`, a product, and multiplication commutes. With flows it is
`sum_t C_t · prod_{s>t}(1 + r_s)`, which does not.

Measured rather than asserted: 20,000 random reorderings of one fixed 360-month record
(the US market, 1996-01…2025-12), seed 20260812, 100% equity throughout, so the multiset
of returns is identical in every draw and *ordering is the only thing that varies*.

| Investor | 5th pct | Median | 95th pct | p95/p5 | Correlation with first-decade return |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Lump sum, no flows | 19.5839 | 19.5839 | 19.5839 | **1.0000** | **0.000** |
| Contributing 1/month | 1,588.76 | 2,303.87 | 3,459.91 | **2.178** | **−0.775** |
| Withdrawing 4%/yr of initial | 8.0508 | 11.9043 | 14.2880 | **1.775** | **+0.775** |

The lump-sum row is the identity, confirmed to the printed precision and to about
1 part in 10¹⁵ in the raw values. The other two rows are the finding: **the same
returns, reordered, span a factor of 2.18 for the contributor and 1.77 for the
withdrawer.** And the correlations are the same magnitude with opposite sign, because
level contributions and level withdrawals are algebraically dual. A bad first decade is
*good* for someone buying through it and bad for someone selling through it.

The consequence for setting the split is that **horizon is the wrong input**. A
30-year accumulator and a 30-year retiree have the same horizon and mirror-image
problems. The input that matters is the schedule of contributions and withdrawals:
sign, size relative to the portfolio, and when they start.

One limitation, stated because it cuts against the result. Permuting imposes an
independent-and-identically-distributed null. It holds the multiset fixed, which is
what makes it the right test of the identity, but it also destroys serial dependence,
so it neither confirms nor denies mean reversion. If real returns mean-revert, real
sequence risk for a withdrawer is smaller than this; if volatility clusters, it is
larger.

## 4. Human capital, honestly

The standard argument: a young investor's future earnings are a large, bond-like asset;
total wealth is financial plus human; so to hold a given fraction of *total* wealth in
equities the *financial* portfolio must be equity-heavy, and it should de-risk as human
capital is spent down. That is the intellectual basis of every target-date glide path
on the market.

It is a real result with a real derivation, and four objections are usually left out.

1. **Labour income is not bond-like for everyone.**
   [Benzoni, Collin-Dufresne and Goldstein (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2007.01271.x),
   *Journal of Finance* 62(5):2123–2167, model labour income and dividends as
   *cointegrated* — a long-run tie that a short-run correlation of roughly zero hides
   completely. Under cointegration the young agent's human capital is **stock-like**,
   because there is time for the tie to bind, and only the older agent's is bond-like,
   because there is not. Their model implies young investors should **short** equities
   and produces **hump-shaped** lifetime holdings. The mechanism the standard argument
   uses can run the other way, and in that paper it does.
2. **Occupation and employer stock decide it, not age.** A tenured public employee and
   a commission-paid salesperson at a cyclical firm do not hold the same asset. Anyone
   holding employer stock in a qualified plan holds a concentrated position whose
   payoff is correlated with their own income at the moment they most need the money.
   The recommendation page already lists occupation, employer exposure and NUA under
   the missing investor-policy inputs; this is what they are for.
3. **Human capital is illiquid and cannot be pledged.** It cannot be sold, borrowed
   against at a usable rate, or rebalanced. Treating it as a bond holding in a mix that
   is then rebalanced quarterly treats an unsellable asset as a tradeable one.
4. **The argument as usually deployed implies leverage that this repository forbids.**
   If human capital is genuinely 80% of a 25-year-old's total wealth and the target
   total-wealth equity share is 60%, the implied financial portfolio is 300% equity.
   The literature knows this and says so; retail practice quietly caps it at 100% and
   keeps the conclusion. [Decision 0004](../decisions/0004-no-sleeve-promoted.md) sets
   leverage to zero, so the capped version is all that is available here — and the
   capped version is just "hold 100% equity while young", which §1.2 already gets from
   the growth objective without any human-capital argument at all.

The one piece that survives all four cleanly is
[Bodie, Merton and Samuelson (1992)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/016518899290044F),
*Journal of Economic Dynamics and Control* 16(3–4):427–449: the ability to vary work effort ex post — to work longer, save
more, or retire later — induces more risk-taking ex ante. That is a statement about
*flexibility*, not about age, and it is the version an application can actually ask
about. **A reader who can absorb a bad decade by working two more years is in a
different position from one who cannot, and the question is answerable.** "How old are
you" is a proxy for it, and a poor one.

Nothing in this section is measured here. It is published theory with published
objections, and it is `not tested` in this repository's vocabulary. It should inform
the *questions* an application asks and must not be turned into a formula that outputs
a weight.

## 5. The drawdown anchor, which is the operational form of the answer

monthly, 1963-07…2025-12, 750 months. Equity is Ken French's US market total return —
the same series and window that produced the 10.80% / 15.40% / −50.3% / 72-month line
in [Experiment 007](long-only-capture.md#the-small-value-corner), reproduced here to
all four printed figures as a check that the pipeline is the same one.

the abstract, the one you would have held through for the *months under water* in the
same row — and read the equity share off the left.

Three warnings that belong beside it and not in a footnote.

- **Drawdown deepens mechanically with sample length**, which is why every rung is
  computed on one window and why no number here may be compared against a drawdown from
  a different window ([`core/drawdown.py`](../../research/src/portfolio_edge/core/drawdown.py)).
- **This is one sample of one country.** −50.3% is what the US did, not a bound. Japan
  1989 and several European markets did materially worse, and this repository has not
  measured them.
- **Bonds shortened the drawdown but did not remove it.** The 90/10 rung's drawdown is
  −45.5%, 4.8 points better than all-equity. The brake works, and it works
  in proportion to how much of it you are holding.

### 5.1 Withdrawals invert part of the table

The drawdown ladder is monotone; the *failure* ladder is not. In real terms — CPI
deflated, level real withdrawal, 30-year horizon, 20,000 reorderings, 748 months of
data (real equity 6.66%/yr, real bond 1.98%/yr) — the probability of running out:

| Real withdrawal | 20% eq | 30% | 40% | 50% | **60%** | 70% | 80% | 90% | 100% |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 3%/yr | 0.07% | 0.03% | **0.03%** | 0.04% | 0.07% | 0.15% | 0.27% | 0.41% | 0.64% |
| 4%/yr | 6.82% | 3.78% | 2.88% | 2.50% | **2.43%** | 2.71% | 3.17% | 3.60% | 4.24% |
| 5%/yr | 47.34% | 32.41% | 22.74% | 17.51% | 14.87% | 13.56% | 13.06% | **12.86%** | 13.16% |
| 6%/yr | 86.77% | 74.97% | 60.77% | 49.05% | 40.96% | 35.16% | 31.31% | **28.74%** | 27.27% |

The minimum walks right as the withdrawal rate rises. At 3% real the safest portfolio
is genuinely the safe one; at 4% the minimum is at 60% equity and a 20% equity
portfolio is nearly three times as likely to fail; at 5% and 6% failure falls almost
all the way to 100% equity. **Above about a 4% real draw, holding too few equities is
the larger risk, not the smaller one.** The mechanism is not subtle: the withdrawal
outruns the return, and no ordering of a 1.98%/yr real bond return supports a 5% real
draw for thirty years.

This is `exploratory`. It is one country, one modelled bond, an iid permutation null,
and a real bond return of 1.98%/yr that no reader should assume forward. It is enough
to show that §1.1's variant C row conflates two cases.

## 6. What the bond side is actually for

[The recommendation](portfolio-recommendation.md) §2 already books bonds correctly:
*"a different benchmark, not an edge"*, sized by risk capacity, and it cites
[Campbell, Pflueger and Viceira (2025)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34323),
*Bond-Stock Comovements*, NBER working paper 34323, October 2025 (revised June 2026),
for the sign flip. Their abstract, retrieved 2026-08-12: *"During the late 20th
Century, nominal government bonds and stocks tended to comove positively, whereas
during the first quarter of the 21st Century they have tended to comove negatively."*

Two corrections to how this repository has been quoting them, and one measurement.

**The era boundaries in §1.1 are not the ones the authors state.** In their own
[February 2026 summary of the paper](https://econbrowser.com/archives/2026/02/guest-contribution-understanding-bond-stock-price-comovements)
(retrieved 2026-08-12), the negative era runs to **2022Q3**, not 2022, and
the positive era that follows runs **2022Q3 to 2024Q2**, not 2023 to 2025Q2. They also
name three earlier sub-periods the recommendation page collapses: negative
1964Q1–1967Q3, positive 1967Q4–1971Q3, and no significant beta 1971Q4–1974Q2. The
picture is not two eras. It is at least six.

**The "US, UK and Eurozone" attribution could not be verified.** The NBER landing page,
the abstract and the authors' own summary are US-only in what they state; the working
paper PDF returned HTTP 403 from two hosts on 2026-08-12. The multi-country claim may
well be in the paper. It is not supported by anything read here, and the recommendation
page should soften it or cite a page number.

**Measured on this repository's own data**, with the modelled ten-year bond and their
sub-period boundaries translated to months:

| Era | Months | Correlation | Beta of bond on equity |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1964-01…1967-09 | 45 | +0.400 | +0.120 |
| 1967-10…1971-09 | 48 | +0.540 | +0.226 |
| 1971-10…1974-06 | 33 | +0.071 | +0.021 |
| **1974-07…1999-12** | 306 | +0.266 | **+0.129** |
| **2000-01…2022-09** | 273 | −0.138 | **−0.055** |
| **2022-10…2024-06** | 21 | +0.258 | **+0.116** |
| 2024-07…2025-12 | 18 | −0.239 | **−0.109** |

The three long eras reproduce the published sign pattern independently. The three short
ones do not, and should not be expected to: 18 to 45 monthly observations carry no
power, and the two earliest disagree with the published sign outright. The final row is
the one worth sitting with. **On the eighteen months since the published sample ends,
this repository's own data says the sign has flipped back to negative again.**

What follows for the split, in the words it deserves: **bonds are held as a risk brake
whose diversification benefit is regime-dependent: the sign changed three times in the
published record and, on this repository's own data, a fourth time since it ended.** That is weaker than "bonds diversify equities". It is
strong enough to keep them — the drawdown ladder in §5 shows the brake working in every
rung — but it is not strong enough to size them from a covariance estimate, and it is
direct evidence for the non-stationarity that §2.1 says is the real argument for a
fractional exposure. Framework open decision 9, on estimation windows and regime
conditioning for the covariance matrix, is the right home for this and remains open.

And the row that should end any conversation about bonds as a floor: **the all-bond
portfolio drew down −25.1% and spent 65 months under water**, deeper and longer than
the 30/70 mix's −17.9% over 41 months. Its worst stretch was 2020-08 to 2023-10, inside
the era when the beta had turned positive again.

## 7. The decision structure

This is the part an application renders. It is a structure, not a number, and it fails
loudly at the step where a forecast is required rather than substituting one.

### Step 1 — Inputs the reader must supply. None can be inferred.

| Input | Why it is needed | Where it is used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The drawdown you would hold through** — depth *and* months under water | The objective is growth *subject to* this. Without it the objective returns a corner | §5 |
| **Cash flows**: sign, size relative to the portfolio, start date, real or nominal | Sequence risk is a cash-flow interaction and its sign flips with the direction of the flow | §3, §5.1 |
| **Withdrawal rate**, if drawing | Decides whether more equity raises or lowers failure risk | §5.1 |
| **Flexibility**: can you work longer, save more, or spend less after a bad decade | The one part of the human-capital argument that survives its objections | §4 |
| **Occupation and employer-stock exposure** | Decides whether human capital is bond-like or stock-like at all | §4 |
| Optionally, an **equity-over-bond premium forecast** | Only needed to run §1.2 forwards. Not needed to run it backwards | §1.2 |

### Step 2 — Arithmetic that runs on them, with no forecast

| Computation | Closed form | Forecast needed? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Growth retained at a fraction of the optimum | `1 − (1 − f)²` | **No** |
| Growth cost of estimating the optimum | `1 / (2T)` | **No** |
| Growth-maximising shrinkage | `S²T / (S²T + 1)` | Needs `S` and a *believed* `T` |
| Premium your chosen weight implies | inverse of `w*` | **No** — this is the honest direction |
| Drawdown and time under water at each weight | one pass over a wealth path | **No**, but it is one historical sample |
| Ruin probability at a withdrawal rate | permutation over a return record | **No**, same caveat |
| Growth-optimal weight `w*` | `Σ⁻¹(μ − μ_b)` form | **Yes.** Stop here and say so |

### Step 3 — The range where being wrong is cheap

Under zero leverage the whole admissible range sits on the left branch of the growth
parabola, so the only error available is underbetting. Against a growth-optimal
exposure of 2.28 (§1.2, at the sample premium, which is an illustration and not a
forecast):

| Equity share | `f` = share / `w*` | Peak excess growth retained |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 40% | 0.18 | 0.32 |
| 60% | 0.26 | 0.45 |
| 80% | 0.35 | 0.58 |
| 100% | 0.44 | 0.68 |

Read that as a warning about the premise, not a recommendation: at the historical
premium, *every* unlevered portfolio is far below the growth-optimal exposure, and the
gradient between 60% and 100% is real. At a forecast premium of 1.2%/yr instead, `w*`
is 0.6 and the same table inverts. **The gradient is entirely a function of the forecast
you are unwilling to make.** Which is the whole reason §1.1 refuses to set the split,
and this page agrees with it.

### Step 4 — The drawdown you are signing up for

§5's table, filtered to the rung the reader chose, showing depth, months under water,
and the same rung's realised return. Displayed with its `as of` date, its window, its
one-country limitation, and the note that the bond column is modelled.

### What the application must not do

- Present §1.2's `w*` without the premium forecast that produced it.
- Add the §1.3 ladder edge to the ~109 bp contractual budget. Different benchmarks;
  [decision 0007](../decisions/0007-application-may-render-research.md) constraint 3
  forbids it and the study code raises.
- Output an equity share from age. §4 is the reason.
- Describe any of §§5–7 as anything but one historical sample.

## Verified, assumed, open

### Verified here

- The `1 − (1 − f)²` retention identity, cross-checked against
  [`core/kelly.py`](../../research/src/portfolio_edge/core/kelly.py)'s independently
  written `growth_rate_vertex` to 1e-15.
- The two-asset `w*` and its inverse, checked against a 20,001-point grid search over
  the same objective at twelve parameter combinations.
- `1/(2T)` and `f* = S²T/(S²T+1)`, checked against a 400,000-draw seeded simulation.
- `(n−1)/(n−3)`, checked against a 400,000-draw simulation at n = 60.
- Permutation invariance of terminal wealth without flows, to floating-point precision.
- The 1963-07…2025-12 market line, 10.80% / 15.40% / −50.3% / 72 months, reproduced
  from Experiment 007 through a different code path.
- The Campbell–Pflueger–Viceira sign pattern in its three long eras, on this
  repository's own data with a modelled bond.

### Assumed on this page, and nowhere else

1. **The ten-year bond total return is modelled from `GS10`,** not measured. No
   total-return bond series exists in this repository. The proxy carries no on-the-run
   premium, no bid/ask, no tax and no index roll rules. Every bond figure inherits it.
2. **Monthly rebalancing** in every constant mix. Experiment 003 priced the policy
   difference at 0.3–1.2 bp/yr in cost and nothing in return, so this is a small
   assumption, but it is one.
3. **`sigma` known and returns Gaussian and independent** in §2.1. Both fail in
   practice; both failures push `f*` down, which is the direction §2.1 already argues.
4. **An iid permutation null** in §3 and §5.1. Deliberate — it is what isolates
   ordering — and it destroys serial dependence.
5. **A 30-year horizon** in §5.1, and CPI-U as the deflator.
6. **Nominal, US, pre-tax throughout §§1, 3, 5 and 6.** §5.1 alone is real.

### Open

- **What drawdown constraint the objective is subject to.** Framework open decision 1.
  This is the binding input and nothing here can supply it.
- **Which estimation window and regime-conditioning scheme the covariance matrix should
  use.** Framework open decision 9, and §6 is now direct evidence that the question
  is live rather than theoretical.
- **Whether risk-constrained Kelly beats fractional Kelly on real returns.** Framework
  open decision 8, still deferred, still correctly.
- **Whether the multi-country bond-stock claim holds.** The paper was unreachable;
  §6 records what was and was not verified.
- **What a non-US drawdown ladder looks like.** −50.3% is a US number and this
  repository has no other equity market's full history loaded.
- **How labour income actually covaries with equities for a given reader.** §4 names
  the question; nothing here answers it.

## Reproducibility

```sh
cd research && uv run python -m portfolio_edge.studies.equity_share
```

- **Equity**: Ken French `F-F_Research_Data_5_Factors_2x3`, monthly `Mkt-RF + RF`,
  decimal, 1963-07…2025-12, 750 observations. Cache sha256 prefix `cbc372481213`.
  French rebuilds the full history from the current vintage on every rebuild, so this
  identifies the file used and makes no point-in-time claim
  ([`data/cache.py`](../../research/src/portfolio_edge/data/cache.py)).
- **Cash**: the `RF` column of the same file.
- **Bond**: FRED `GS10`, monthly constant-maturity yield, decimal per year, converted to
  a total return by pricing a semiannual par bond struck at last month's yield and
  repricing 9 years 11 months of it at this month's. Modelled.
- **Inflation**: FRED `CPIAUCSL`, index 1982-1984 = 100. Its coverage ends two months
  before the equity series, so §5.1 runs on 748 months rather than 750.
- **Seed** 20260812, **20,000** permutation draws per cell, 360-month horizons.
- 42 tests in `research/tests/unit/test_studies_equity_share.py`; the full research
  suite passes `as of 2026-08-12`. No experiment was run and no ledger entry was
  written: this is a study, not an experiment, and it decides nothing that a frozen
  specification would have to adjudicate.

## Consequence for this repository

1. **§1.1 keeps the refusal and gains the reason.** The recommendation page should not
   set the split and this page does not ask it to. What changes is the account of *why*
   the variants exist: not "risk capacity" as an undefined faculty, but a drawdown
   constraint that the objective is explicitly subject to and that nobody has supplied.
   §1.1 now links here for the arithmetic.
2. **Variant C should be split.** "Under 10 years" and "withdrawals have begun" have
   opposite answers above a 4% real draw. That is a correction to §1.1, not a new
   recommendation, and it is made in place.
3. **The bond–stock citation needs tightening.** The era boundaries and the
   three-currency-area attribution in §1.1 are not supported by anything reachable on
   2026-08-12. §6 states what is.
4. **An application may render §7 and must render it whole.** Every input in step 1,
   the stop-here marker at step 2's last row, and the drawdown table with its
   limitations. A calculator that outputs an equity share from an age or a risk
   questionnaire is the thing this page exists to prevent.
5. **Nothing here reopens decision 0004.** Leverage stays at zero. The observation that
   the growth objective would like more than 100% equity is an observation about the
   objective, not a case for borrowing: the four conditions that would reopen capital
   efficiency are unchanged and none is met.
6. **`studies/equity_share.py` is the executable record.** Any change to a number on
   this page changes a test, by construction.
